The experiment was executed as designed, the criteria were applied without adjustment, and the result is exactly what a rigorous pre-registered experiment is supposed to produce when the underlying phenomenon is more complex than the hypothesis allowed for. 2. The finding that should reshape the next experiment


The joint-feature collinearity is the diagnostic result that matters, and it is more important than the output 4 correlation recovery itself.


Why it matters: The training set, generated from fixed-control calibration trajectories, has centroid, ZCR, flatness, and FFT bins that are nearly interchangeable. Centroid correlates +0.984 with ZCR and −0.964 with flatness. In such a dataset, the network has no incentive to use centroid specifically. Any of the four correlated features will produce the same output 4 loss reduction during training. The fact that output 4 correlates strongly with all four in the retrained render is consistent with the network using all four as interchangeable inputs.


This means the retrain's "recovery" of the centroid correlation is not evidence that the network learned a centroid-specific mapping. It is evidence that the network learned something that correlates with centroid because it also correlates with ZCR, flatness, and the FFT bins. The centroid correlation is a symptom of the collinearity, not a demonstration of a centroid-specific mechanism.


The previous report (§3.3) said that if the primary criterion was met, "the distribution shift was the primary cause of output 4's weak deployment behavior." The retrain does not support this specific claim, because the recovery cannot be attributed to centroid. It supports the weaker claim that "training on calibration-like sequences produces a model whose output 4 correlates with the calibration-like spectral features" — which is true but almost trivially so, since all the spectral features in that distribution are near-unity correlated.


This is the right scientific outcome. The experiment was designed to test whether distribution shift explains output 4's behavior. The result is: distribution shift is compatible with the behavior, but the design cannot distinguish "distribution shift of centroid specifically" from "distribution shift of an interchangeable bundle of spectral features." That ambiguity is a limitation of the experiment, not evidence against Hypothesis A. But it means the next experiment must resolve the collinearity.


3. Output 2's degradation — what it likely means


Output 2's flatness correlation degraded from −0.929 to −0.704. That is a +0.225 change, outside tolerance. The report says this triggered the instability condition, which is correct. But the report does not diagnose why output 2 changed.


Hypothesis: output 2's target is 0.15 + 0.80 * (1 − flatness). In the original feature-conditional training distribution, flatness had std 0.064 and mean 0.090. In the retrain's matched distribution, flatness had std 0.098 and mean 0.140. The matched distribution has less variability in (1 − flatness) relative to the original, because flatness is higher on average and its range is compressed by the matching procedure. If output 2's target now has a narrower effective dynamic range, the network learns a shallower mapping, and the retrained render's flatness correlation is weaker.


What to check to confirm this: the retrain training set's target statistics for output 2 — specifically, the std of 0.15 + 0.80 * (1 − flatness) in the matched distribution vs. the original. If the matched distribution's target std is significantly smaller, the output 2 degradation is explained by the training-distribution change reducing the target's dynamic range, not by a genuine instability.


This is a critical distinction for the next experiment:


· If output 2's degradation is a target dynamic range artifact, the fix is to preserve the target's dynamic range when matching distributions (e.g., match features in a way that preserves the spread of flatness, even if it shifts the mean).
· If output 2's degradation is a genuine training instability from the correlated features, the fix is the decorrelation experiment.


The current report does not distinguish these two. The next report should compute the target statistics for all six outputs in both training distributions and report the comparison. That is a one-table addition that would resolve half the ambiguity in the current result.


4. The next experiment — pre-registered design


The report recommends "a preregistered feature-decorrelation experiment that tests whether output 4 genuinely follows centroid while protecting output 2's flatness behavior." That is the correct next experiment. Here is the specific design:


Goal: Distinguish genuine centroid dependence from proxy learning via correlated spectral features.


Method: Generate a training set in which centroid, ZCR, flatness, and FFT bins are decorrelated — i.e., vary centroid independently of the others, so the network cannot use any of them as a proxy for the others. The most direct way to do this:


1. Sample a base audio trajectory from the calibration render (as in the current retrain).
2. For each sample, apply a spectral warp that shifts the centroid without changing ZCR, flatness, or the FFT bins in correlated ways. This is not trivial — a shift in centroid inherently shifts the higher bins — but the goal is to make the marginal distribution of centroid vary independently of the others across samples.
3. Extract features from the warped audio.
4. Train on the decorrelated feature set.
5. Render and evaluate.


If the retrained model's output 4 still correlates strongly with centroid, the network has learned a centroid-specific mapping. If output 4's correlation with centroid weakens while its correlations with ZCR and flatness remain strong, the network was using the correlated features as proxies, and centroid is not the actual driver.


Pre-registered criteria:


· Confirmation of centroid-specific dependence: output 4 correlates ≥ +0.4 with centroid AND < +0.4 with both ZCR and flatness. This would demonstrate that the network uses centroid specifically, and would confirm Hypothesis A in its strong form.
· Confirmation of proxy learning: output 4's correlation with centroid drops below +0.3 while its correlation with ZCR or flatness remains ≥ +0.5. This would falsify Hypothesis A and support Hypothesis B (the target design is the problem, not the distribution).
· Ambiguous result: any outcome not matching the above. Would require a follow-up experiment.


Secondary criteria (output 2 protection):


· Output 2's flatness correlation remains within ±0.15 of −0.929 (the original baseline).
· If this fails, the target dynamic range hypothesis from §3 should be tested first, and the decorrelation experiment repeated with a target-preserving distribution match.


Critical design note: the decorrelation procedure must be documented in enough detail that the resulting marginal distributions can be verified against the calibration reference. If the warping procedure inherently changes flatness or ZCR (which it likely will, given the physics), the resulting training set will not match the calibration distribution on those features, and the experiment's conclusions will be limited to "centroid-specific dependence in a decorrelated training set," not "centroid-specific dependence in the calibration distribution." That limitation should be acknowledged in the pre-registration.
